# Someone Clicked the Nudge. Did Their Life Actually Improve?

*Anicca Research / Manager Masterclass P12-2 / November 8, 2026. Editorial draft, not yet published.*

Every morning an AI reminds someone to take a walk. Their step count increases. A dashboard reports that the intervention is working.

But what if repeated messages are exhausting? What if someone starts depending on prompts to make routine decisions? What if a small gain in walking comes with worse sleep or reduced autonomy?

**Behavioral response is not the same thing as durable human benefit.**

## Short-term effects are not long-term outcomes

Researchers developing Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) explicitly distinguish proximal outcomes, such as activity in the next hour, from distal outcomes, such as longer-term health or functioning. Micro-randomized trials can estimate the immediate effect of support at repeated decision points. A separate longer-term evaluation is still needed before claiming an improvement in everyday life.

## The dropout problem

Imagine a purely fictional trial randomizing 100 people to Manager and 100 to an ordinary-support control. At follow-up, 80 in the Manager arm are observed, with 60 successes. In the control arm, 90 are observed, with 54 successes.

Among observed participants, success is 75% versus 60%—a fifteen-percentage-point difference.

Yet the outcomes of 20 treatment participants and 10 controls are unknown. If missing outcomes are binary and we make no assumptions about their values, the full-arm difference could range from **minus 4 to plus 26 percentage points**.

This range is not a confidence interval, and the participants do not exist. It illustrates how an apparently compelling success story can depend on whose outcomes are missing.

Cochrane's Risk of Bias 2 framework explicitly treats missing outcomes, deviations from intended interventions, and selective reporting as distinct risks to trial validity.

## Measure harm, not just engagement

The WHO-5 is an established five-item self-report wellbeing measure, but it measures a specific construct and carries usage conditions. Wellbeing cannot simply be inferred from clicks, retention, or revenue.

Published research into meditation-related adverse experiences reminds us that potentially helpful practices can also involve distress or functional impairment for some participants. Studies differ in design and population; their reported rates must not be treated as a single universal risk.

Consent, the ability to stop, burdens, adverse outcomes, and consequences for daily functioning should be measured separately from short-term task performance.

## An honest study report

CONSORT 2025 asks randomized trial reports to address harms assessment, missing data, participant flow, actual intervention delivery, and data access. These are not footnotes to hide after a positive headline. They are the basis of a trustworthy claim.

Manager's goal should be a measurable, bounded improvement in someone's chosen life outcomes—not a higher notification-response rate mislabeled as wellbeing.
