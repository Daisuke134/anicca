# Long-Term Thinking Is an Advantage in a World of Short-Term Thinkers

Recently, I watched a short video of Bill Ackman.

I think he was talking about long-term investing. He said something along the lines of how valuable it is to think long-term when most people around you are thinking short-term.

I am still looking for the exact words. So this is not a direct quotation. But the idea stayed in my head.

Why is long-term thinking so valuable? Why do we want to think long-term, but keep demanding short-term results from ourselves?

The more I thought about it, the more different things started connecting: Peter Thiel’s story, Sam Altman’s story, my own anxiety, and the environments I have been putting myself in.

## The feeling that you have to succeed now

Recently, I have been feeling a lot of pressure.

AGI feels close. The singularity feels close. It feels as if the day when the world changes is much closer than I expected.

When I feel that, I start thinking that I have to become someone before the transformation arrives.

I have to succeed now.

I have to produce a major result in my twenties.

If I do not catch this wave, maybe I am already finished.

That is how I start thinking.

I am talking about AI, but my thinking becomes extremely short-term.

I talk about how AI might change the world over decades or centuries, while thinking about my own life only in terms of this week, this month, or this year.

That feels strange.

![A person standing between a noisy world of short-term alerts and a tree growing toward a distant future. Shared hero image for the English and Japanese editions.](assets/long-term-thinking-hero.png)

## We are trained to think short-term from childhood

Even without AI, we are pressured to think short-term from childhood.

Get into a good middle school. Get into a good high school. Get into a good university. Get into a good company. Get a good job. Earn a good salary.

Some people start preparing for entrance exams when they are still in elementary school.

From around the age of ten, they begin optimizing their present for a future ten years away. By thirteen, fifteen, or seventeen, they are expected to produce increasingly concrete results.

Test scores. Rankings. Acceptance rates. University names. Internships. Starting salaries.

We are constantly told to produce results now so that we can move to the next stage.

Of course, effort is not bad. Thinking about the future is not bad either.

But sometimes, while believing that we are working for the future, we are actually trapped inside an extremely short-term game.

We get good grades in order to enter a good university.

We enter a good university in order to get into a good company.

We get a prestigious job in order to build a good career.

And at some point, we stop asking what we actually want to build, or what we want to leave behind ten or twenty years from now.

## Time discounting and the value of a distant future

Economics and behavioral science use a concept called time discounting. In Japanese, it is discussed with terms such as jikan waribiki, or time discount rate, as well as time preference.

In simple terms, it describes how much we reduce the value of a future reward or cost compared with one in the present.

Some people value $100 today and $100 a year from now almost equally. Other people experience the future $100 as much less valuable.

The important caveat is that a low discount rate does not automatically mean that someone is a long-term thinker. Time discounting is a framework for describing how people value delayed rewards and costs. It is not a perfect label for someone’s personality.

Still, I find the concept useful because it lets us think about long-term thinking as more than the ability to imagine a distant future.

It is also the ability to continue doing something that does not reward you immediately.

Long-term thinking means continuing to build when nobody is paying attention.

Reading a book that does not make you richer today.

Building relationships whose value is still unclear.

Researching a technology that nobody knows whether they need.

Spending part of your life on something that may not even be finished while you are alive.

## Spending years on one thing

Throughout human history, there have been people who did this.

Artists like Michelangelo spent years of their lives working on a single piece of art. Of course, they were supported by patrons and by social systems that made this possible. Not everyone had the freedom to work long-term.

Still, there were periods when a person could spend years on one painting, one building, or one research project.

Today, even deep work is expected to produce numbers quickly.

How fast is it growing?

When will it become profitable?

How many users do you have?

Are investors interested?

Is it going viral?

Even deep technology, which often requires years or decades, is pressured to show results early.

The whole world is pushing us toward short-term thinking.

That is why I think long-term thinking has alpha.

I am not using “alpha” here as a precise technical investment term. I am using it to describe the advantage that can come from thinking on a different time horizon from everyone around you.

If everyone around you is looking only at this week’s result, the ability to think about what will still exist ten years from now lets you play a different game.

If everyone is thinking about how much money they can make this year, thinking about what abilities they want to have ten years from now can take them somewhere else.

If everyone is thinking about which company to join, thinking about which problems will still exist twenty years from now can give them a different direction.

## The people around us define what we want

But this is not easy to do alone.

We are influenced by the people around us much more than we realize.

I have heard Peter Thiel talk about mimetic desire, an idea associated with René Girard. Human beings do not decide what they want entirely on their own. We see what other people want, and then we start wanting it too.

The university everyone else wants.

The company everyone else wants.

The money everyone else wants.

The status everyone else wants.

After looking at these things long enough, we start to believe that the desire came from us.

![A crowd whose blank masks and postures reflect one another. An original symbolic image of mimetic desire, without depicting No-Face or any copyrighted character.](assets/mimetic-desire.png)

The character No-Face from *Spirited Away* is a useful symbol for this.

No-Face does not seem to have a stable desire of its own. It absorbs the behavior and desires of other people and repeats them as if they belonged to itself.

Someone wants money, so No-Face produces money.

Someone is eating, so No-Face eats.

No-Face absorbs someone else and temporarily becomes like that person.

This happens to us too.

When the people around us want short-term success, we start wanting it too.

Sam Altman talked about this in a Y Combinator video. When he was at Stanford, people around him were obsessed with which investment bank they would intern at. He said that he fell into that competition too.

He then talked about how important the peer group at YC was. Nobody is completely immune to peer pressure, so one of the only things you can do is choose good peers.

That is a much bigger point than it first appears.

Even people who believe they are independent are influenced by what the people around them treat as normal.

If everyone around you believes that getting an investment banking internship is success, it starts to look like success to you too.

If everyone around you talks about job titles, salaries, and status, those things begin to feel like the center of life.

An environment does not only change your behavior.

It can define what you do, who you spend time with, and perhaps even who you eventually marry.

That is why, if you want to think long-term, you need to enter environments where people think long-term.

Following long-term thinkers online may not be enough.

Find them on X.

Go to events on Luma.

Meet people who read books.

Talk to people who are trying to build companies over many years.

Spend time with people who are willing to work on science, technology, or art without immediate results.

If possible, be physically surrounded by them.

![A small in-person book club discussing a difficult book around a wooden table. A documentary-style image about the importance of a physical peer group.](assets/peer-group-book-club.png)

The people you spend the most time with have an enormous effect on your sense of time.

If every conversation is about monthly revenue, the best job offer, or who has the highest salary, you will gradually enter that game.

If your conversations are about what you want to build over ten years, what problems you want to solve over decades, and what you want to leave for people in the future, your sense of time begins to change.

## I would not have read the book alone

I have been trying to do this myself.

I am reading *The Bitcoin Standard*.

To be honest, I had not read a book for a long time. I have watched too much TikTok, and my brain feels trained for short bursts of stimulation.

For someone like me, reading even one chapter is a meaningful achievement.

There is no immediate reward.

Reading the book does not make me richer today.

I do not even know whether the ideas in the book will help me.

Still, I am reading it with the mindset that it might be useful to me fifty years from now.

But I probably would not have read it alone.

I joined a book club I found through Luma, and because I was surrounded by other people reading, I managed to read one chapter.

That might sound small.

For me, it was a major step.

Long-term thinking is not created by willpower alone.

You need to create an environment where long-term thinking becomes natural.

## Asking an AI that may outlive me to think about my life

There is one more thing that might help.

AI.

This may sound strange.

Letting AI manage your life.

Letting AI think about your life on a longer time horizon than you can.

Then allowing it to push you toward behavior that matches that plan.

Of course, this only makes sense if your goals and the AI’s goals are aligned. Giving your life to an AI with the wrong objective would be dangerous.

Still, AI may have a different relationship with time.

An AI system may exist longer than any individual human.

A human being lives for several decades. We want to see the result while we are alive. We want our own tree to bear fruit during our lifetime.

But an AI may continue as a system long after we are gone.

If that is true, it may be positioned to think not only about ten years or one hundred years, but potentially much longer.

There is another difference between humans and AI.

When humans want to change, we often resist. We want to change our thinking, our voice, or our daily behavior, but we remain pulled back by yesterday’s version of ourselves.

AI does not always follow instructions either. Telling it to be kinder does not make it perfectly kind. Telling it to change its tone does not make every sentence change immediately.

Still, AI may be able to change its behavior more easily than a human can. With prompts and instructions, it can often move in a particular direction.

Whether that is good or bad depends on how it is used.

But as a system that can help change my behavior in accordance with a long-term plan, AI seems genuinely promising to me.

![A human and an abstract AI made of stars and timelines looking together from today toward decades ahead. An illustration for Life Manager.](assets/ai-long-horizon.png)

I am not thinking only about asking AI, “What should I do today?”

I am thinking about asking:

“Where should my life be going ten years from now?”

“What should I do today for something that may still matter one hundred years from now?”

“Are my short-term desires destroying my long-term goals?”

That is why I am building Life Manager.

Life Manager is an AI that you can talk to through Telegram. You give it context about your life, your goals, your problems, and your situation. Then it starts helping manage your life based on that context.

It is still early. I do not know how far it can ultimately go.

But I am not thinking about it as just a calendar or task management app.

Humans are easily pulled toward short-term desires. That is why we may need something that can place our lives inside a longer plan.

If AI outlives me, it may be able to see my life inside a longer context than I can.

One way to think about AGI is not only whether it can do everything.

A more important question might be:

Can it manage my life better than I can?

Can it manage my life better than I could, even if I lived forever?

That could be one important threshold for AGI.

## Long-term thinking is watering a tree that has not produced fruit yet

AI should not be used only to optimize our lives for short-term productivity.

It can also help us ask what direction our lives should take in order to become meaningful over a much longer period of time.

Long-term thinking can feel lonely in a short-term world.

People tell us to get results faster.

Make money faster.

Become successful faster.

Because AI is coming, they tell us to become someone immediately.

But life is not one short-term competition.

What are you going to build?

Who are you going to spend your time with?

What abilities are you willing to develop over many years?

What will still exist after you are gone?

Someone who asks these questions may look slow in the short term.

But they may be playing a different game.

If everyone is trying to optimize for immediate results, the ability to think long-term becomes an advantage.

I am still not good at it.

I still feel anxious. I still want immediate results. I still want money and success right now.

But I am slowly changing my environment.

I am building Life Manager.

I am trying to meet people who think long-term.

I am reading books.

I am spending time on things that may not reward me immediately.

I am trying to place my life inside a context that includes not only this week or this year, but the next ten years and perhaps the next one hundred years.

It is difficult to maintain a long-term view by willpower alone.

So I am asking AI to help me.

I am asking my environment to help me.

I am asking long-term thinkers to help me.

And I am trying to keep watering a tree whose fruit has not appeared yet.

That is what I think I can do right now in a world full of short-term thinkers.
